## TL;DR

- **Copper** is a CRM that lives in Gmail, Calendar, and Drive, auto-building records from email history. Basic $23/seat, Professional $59, Business $99 on annual billing — and Copper's own site says 63% of customers start on Professional.
- **Solve CRM** has one plan at $39/user/month with the full web and iOS apps included, no feature gates, and a four-user minimum that sets a $156/month floor.
- Both are Google-first and both handle post-sale client work. Copper is the stronger sales instrument; Solve is the cheaper flat rate for a services firm that mostly needs organization.

## The same starting premise

Neither of these wants to be a standalone destination. Copper's entire design is Gmail — you work the pipeline from a sidebar, and contact records populate themselves from email and calendar history so reps never have to switch context. Copper GPT adds natural-language querying over that data, plus an email rewriter and template generation.

Solve takes the Google commitment just as seriously without living inside the inbox: two-way Gmail sync, Calendar scheduling, and Google Sheets as the reporting surface. That last point is a real constraint — Solve's in-app reporting is limited, and custom analysis means pushing data to Sheets. Copper's reporting is more capable in-product, though the repo notes it still trails Salesforce and Pipedrive for complex sales orgs.

Both are the wrong purchase on Microsoft 365. Copper on Outlook means paying for an inbox integration your seats cannot use; Solve loses the Workspace tie that justifies it.

## Price shapes: a ladder versus a flat rate

Copper's $23 Basic is largely an anchor. It covers Gmail sync, contacts, one pipeline, and basic reports — no workflow automation, no multiple pipelines, and not the integration surface most teams assume comes standard. The moment you want an automated nudge after five days of silence, you are on Professional at $59. Business at $99 buys higher automation limits, lead scoring, and finer permissions, which matters for a 30-person org with overlapping territories and is $480 a month of unused headroom for a ten-person agency.

Solve has no ladder to climb. One plan, $39, everything included — no Professional tier withholding reporting, no Enterprise tier gating the API. What it has instead is a floor:

| Actual users | Billed seats | Monthly | Effective per person |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 4 | $156 | $156 |
| 2 | 4 | $156 | $78 |
| 4 | 4 | $156 | $39 |
| 12 | 12 | $468 | $39 |

Copper publishes no seat minimum, so a one-person subscription is possible — worth confirming at checkout, since minimums sometimes appear on negotiated annual contracts.

## What each one costs at the tier you would really buy

Compare Solve's $39 against Copper Professional's $59, since that is where Copper becomes the tool people describe when they recommend it. At ten seats, that is $390/month versus $590/month — Solve roughly a third cheaper for a team of that shape.

The $20 gap buys workflow automation, multiple pipelines, bulk email, and the wider integration surface. If follow-up discipline is your bottleneck, that is money well spent. If your problem is keeping client engagements organized rather than chasing deals, you are paying for machinery Solve does not pretend to have.

Neither vendor offers a light or read-only seat, which hits both the same way: an ops person pulling reports monthly costs full freight. On Copper that is $59, on Solve $39, and on both it creates pressure toward shared logins that is bad for audit trails.

## Terms, discounts, and evaluation

Copper discounts up to 26% for annual commitment, widening as you climb — $6 a seat on Basic, $10 on Professional, $35 on Business. That Business gap is aggressive enough to read as a signal about which accounts Copper wants locked in. While you are piloting, take the monthly rate for a quarter; the flexibility beats $10 a seat. There is no free plan, only a no-card trial.

Solve advertises no annual discount at all, which is unusual in this bracket — monthly appears to simply be the price. Ask sales directly before assuming the standard 15 to 20% exists. Its trial is 14 days with no card, after which the meter starts at four seats regardless of how many people you have.

Two things worth getting in writing. From Solve: storage limits, which are not published and matter if you plan to keep client files in the system. From Copper: nothing at signup, but understand that migrating off later means untangling records auto-created from email history, which is messier than exporting a hand-maintained database.

## Record models tell you who each is for

Solve tracks contacts, companies, projects, properties, and cases in one system, with the interface organized around activity and follow-up rather than deal stages. For a consultancy, an HVAC business, or a non-profit, the deal is not the terminal object — deliverables, recurring work, and client files continue afterward, and Solve models that natively.

Copper has a project management layer too, but its center of gravity stays on the pipeline: opportunities, leads, and the sales motion that produces them. Its weakest tiers are the ones that strip those objects out, which is precisely why so few customers stay on Basic.

## Who should pick what

- **Google-native B2B sales teams of five to forty** → Copper Professional. The Gmail sidebar is why reps open it.
- **Services firms of four to twenty-five managing ongoing client work** → Solve, for the flat rate and the project layer.
- **Solo operators and pairs** → Copper Basic or Professional; Solve's minimum makes it two to four times its sticker price per head.
- **Teams whose bottleneck is follow-up discipline** → Copper, for the automation Solve does not offer.
- **Buyers who want zero tier archaeology and a predictable invoice** → Solve.
- **Anyone on Microsoft 365** → neither; the Google integration is the entire argument for both.

## Bottom line

These two are closer than most matchups on this site, and the split is clean. Copper is the better sales tool and costs $20 more a seat at the tier that matters, justified if automation and multiple pipelines are things you will actually use. Solve is the better value for a small Google-based services firm that wants one predictable price and a system where client projects are first-class — provided you have four people. Under four, Copper is the only one of the two that prices honestly for your size.
